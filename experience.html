MASTER'S THESIS STUDENT POWERTRAIN SYSTEM – FUNCTION DEVELOPMENT

HV Energy Management

Development and validation of selected supervisory-control functions within a modular high-voltage energy-management architecture for electrified powertrains.

CONTEXT

Charging an electrified vehicle is not simply a matter of sending all available grid power to the battery. Active vehicle loads, battery limits, state of charge and safety conditions all compete for the same electrical resources.

The thesis focused on selected supervisory functions that make these decisions explicit and testable inside a modular MATLAB/Simulink architecture.

WHAT I DID

01 Charging function

Developed rule-based logic to allocate external grid power between the traction battery, HV auxiliary loads and LV auxiliary demand.

02 Battery-aware operation

Used SoC and battery power limits to determine when battery support was permitted and when further discharge had to be prevented.

03 Load prioritization

Implemented deterministic allocation and shedding between battery thermal demand and comfort demand when available HV auxiliary power was limited.

04 HV safety logic

Implemented fault-triggered safe-state handling and DC-link discharge behavior for crash, interlock, insulation and contactor fault inputs.

DEFINE BEHAVIOR → IMPLEMENT → TEST → ANALYZE

RESULTS & FINDINGS

01

The implemented logic produced the intended charging states and transitions.

The selected validation cases covered Low-SoC Protection, Battery Discharge Support, transition between those states, and charging up to the target SoC.

02

The wider operating sweep exposed a clear energy-management trade-off.

At low SoC, battery protection reduced the amount of auxiliary demand that could be satisfied. At higher SoC, battery support improved auxiliary availability but reduced the final battery SoC.

03

Load priorities directly determined shedding behavior.

Because battery thermal demand had priority over comfort demand, constrained power was assigned to thermal management first while comfort power was reduced.

04

Safety behavior correctly overrode normal energy-management operation.

In the selected simulated fault cases, the controller generated the intended safe-state request and activated the discharge sequence.

ENGINEERING TAKEAWAY

The work showed the strength and the limit of deterministic rule-based control. Its decisions are transparent and directly traceable to thresholds, states and priorities, which makes functional validation straightforward. Those same rules, however, do not guarantee an energy-optimal solution.

Scope

Functional development and simulation-based validation of selected supervisory functions. The thesis does not claim complete vehicle-level validation, optimality or automotive safety certification.

From requirements to verification.

The internship introduced me to the automotive function-development workflow around charging and high-voltage energy management. The work repeatedly moved between understanding the system, documenting expected behavior, implementing functions and checking whether the model responded as intended.

CONTEXT

I started by building an understanding of HV energy-management systems, their major components and the communication paths between the battery-management, energy-management and charging systems.

The work then moved into automotive requirements engineering and the implementation and verification of charging-related functions.

WHAT I DID

01 System understanding

Researched HV energy-management architectures, their components and the communication between BMS, EMS and charging management.

02 Requirements engineering

Created and repeatedly updated functional requirements for HV-energy-management and charging-related behavior.

03 Simulink development

Developed and updated EMM and charging-related functions in MATLAB/Simulink as requirements and understanding evolved.

04 Verification & test design

Verified behavior with dummy signals and scenarios and developed detailed test cases, expected reactions and boundary-condition tests.

RESEARCH → REQUIREMENTS → MODEL → VERIFY → UPDATE

OUTCOMES

01

The requirements evolved with the engineering understanding.

Requirement documentation was revised repeatedly as new findings, model behavior and functional changes became available.

02

The Simulink functions were verified iteratively rather than only at the end.

Dummy signals and defined scenarios were used throughout development to check whether the functions responded according to their intended behavior.

03

Testing expanded from nominal behavior toward edge conditions.

The later internship work included detailed test cases, expected responses, AC/DC charging differences and boundary-condition scenarios.

04

Analysis fed back into the next model revision.

Test results were documented and analyzed to identify improvement opportunities, followed by additional requirement and model updates.

ENGINEERING TAKEAWAY

The strongest pattern in this role was iteration. Requirements were not written once and forgotten, and the model was not implemented once and considered finished. Requirements, implementation, verification, test results and model updates repeatedly informed one another.
